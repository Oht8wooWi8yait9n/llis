NASA Lesson #30601: SAC-D (Aquarius/Satélite de Aplicaciones Científicas) DC/DC Converter On-orbit Failures Likely Due To Overtesting Prior to Usage

Lesson ID: #30601 (Database ID: 30601)
Date: 2011-06-01
Organization: Goddard Space Flight Center
Program Phase: Not Specified
Topic / Category: Integration and Testing, Missions and Systems Requirements Definition, Parts, Materials, & Processes, Test & Verification, Reliability, Planning of requirements verification processes
Source: View Official NASA LLIS Entry (live system)

Driving Event / Description

The SAC-D (Aquarius/Satélite de Aplicaciones Científicas) mission hosting NASA’s Aquarius instrument launched in June of 2011 on a 3-year mission.  The project (managed by the Argentine Space Agency) selected commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) DC/DC converters (direct current to direct current power converters) to achieve some necessary performance capability from a manufacturer with whom GSFC had limited experience, and screened the hybrids to Level 1 requirements (commensurate with Class A missions, although the mission was more aligned with Class C or D), MIL-PRF-38534, Class K.  About a year into operations, the first converter failed on-orbit.  Multiple converter failures occurred over time, but fault-tolerance in the system design maintained system function over the required lifetime. Approximately four years after launch, one of the remaining critical converters failed, ending the mission.  The assessment shortly after the failure indicated that there were some features in DPA samples that would be considered rejectable, and the failures were attributed to workmanship in the parts.  Years later, GSFC SMA reviewed the data and noted that the DPA discrepancies are simply a violation of the MIL SPEC and do not indicate a credible linkage to multiple on-orbit failures over the one-year to four-year duration.

 

There were two credible possibilities to consider to explain the string of failures: (1) accumulated radiation effects associated with weak designs (converters failed within multiple circuit applications and these particular converters were radiation hardened) or (2) handling or overtesting prior to usage, reducing the remaining lifetime of the parts.  Item (1) is possible, but the fact that this occurred in multiple applications, it becomes very unlikely.  Review of the specification sheet indicates a tolerance to 500g constant acceleration of the parts.  However, MIL-PRF-38534 demands a 3000g constant acceleration test, which was ultimately performed on all of the parts as a screen, regardless of the limits in the parts’ datasheet.  Hybrid DC/DC converters have large magnetics connected to an alumina substrate that is subject to wear under excessive mechanical stress, so this is the most likely explanation for premature wear of the parts.  It should be noted that there was no relevance of a 3000g constant acceleration test for the usage of these parts.  The test was originally designed in order to test bond wires for leaded transistors, and since extrapolated to cover hybrids.

Lesson(s) Learned

  • Testing (screening) parts to stress levels well above the application and close to or above the specification limits for an extended period of time may substantially reduce the lifetime of the parts with minimal benefit (application overstress).
  • Testing (screening) parts to stress levels well above the parts data sheet limits may substantially reduce the lifetime of the parts, whether there is a perceived benefit or not (datasheet overstress).
  • Prior to performing either application or datasheet overstress to parts for screening purposes, the risk of overstress to the parts in the project context should be evaluated and captured. 

Recommendation(s)

  1. Carefully consider the relevance and compatibility, with the manufacturer in the loop, when applying MIL-SPEC screening and qualification processes to COTS parts.
  2. Avoid applying screening levels to parts that go outside of datasheet limits without endorsement/approval/recommended from the manufacturer.
  3. Avoid applying screening levels that greatly exceed the mission application (survival) requirements, as in many cases, such testing will unnecessarily increase risk or reduce the lifetime of the parts.
