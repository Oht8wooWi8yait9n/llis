NASA Lesson #30004: Relationship of Government and Contractor Risk Management Processes

Lesson ID: #30004 (Database ID: 30004)
Date: 2018-04-05
Organization: Langley Research Center
Program Phase: Implementation
Topic / Category: Procurement, Small Business & Industrial Relations, Program and Project Management, Risk Management / Assessment
Source: View Official NASA LLIS Entry (live system)

Driving Event / Description

Throughout the project, it was repeatedly determined that the risk management approach at the time was not adequate for ensuring proper government management of instrument development risks. In the initial risk management approach, the government relied primarily on the contractor to identify and manage instrument development risks within their corporate process. This approach was ineffective for RBI and a second and then third approach was implemented. The characteristics of each approach, the observed effectiveness, and the project’s response to the situation are summarized in the table below.

 

Approach 1

Characteristics

Effectiveness

Response

The government (i.e., the RBI project) maintained risks to level 1 requirements, project success criteria and spacecraft (which was outside the scope of the instrument contractor’s work). The instrument prime contractor maintained risks associated with instrument development.

There were various problems with this approach. The contractor risk management process was driven by corporate constraints, as the contractor process required allocation of reserve to any identified risk. This drove the contractor to dismiss or close risks they were not planning to mitigate or identify cost reserves against. There was an inherent incentive for the contractor to underestimate risk.

In the Delta System Requirements Review Report, the Standing Review Board (SRB) stated that:

The SRB had concern understanding the Project’s perspective on the seriousness of risks.  Project risks include only those for which the Project controls the potential mitigations. [Contractor Name redacted] risks include only those for which [Contractor Name redacted] controls the potential mitigations.  It was unclear as to relative ranking of Project and [Contractor Name redacted] risks and hence, what are the most important risks? In addition, the absence of Project-level focused risks associated with items such as delivery schedule(s) give the impression that those risks are not getting the attention that they deserve.

 

Approach 2

Characteristics

Effectiveness

Response

The government imported as-delivered contractor risks and re-scored them against government risk scoring criteria. The ownership of the contractor risks remained with the contractor, and these risks were designated as contractor risks in the government risk register. The government attempted to influence the contractor handling and management of their owned risks through participation.

This allowed the project to see a rank order of risks carried by both the contractor and government, with the contractor risks scored from the government perspective, which provided greater ongoing awareness to the risks owned by the contractor. This change, however, did not result in significant improvement to the government’s mitigation of the risks owned by the contractor, and did not expand the government owned risks beyond those that had the potential impact to Level 1 requirements, project success criteria, or the spacecraft.  This process also resulted in dual tracking of contractor risk information, which was an added effort to the risk management process and required considerable coordination between the government and contractor to maintain understanding of risks and configuration control risk information. At that time, the contractor remained a supporting part of the government-led Risk Management Boards, which facilitated contractor input into government risk management discussions and activities relating to risk.

Between Preliminary Design Review (PDR) and Critical Design Review (CDR), it was determined that the risk management approach at the time was not adequate for ensuring proper government management of instrument development risks.  At this time, the risk management approach was updated to distinctly separate the government process from the contractor risk management processes.

 

Approach 3

Characteristics

Effectiveness

Response

In the third approach, the government began maintaining a government-only risk register (and risk management board). The government’s risk identification and management was also expanded to capture and address instrument development risks in addition to the previously tracked potential impact to Level 1 requirements, project success criteria, or the spacecraft risks.  From the process transition perspective, all previous contractor risks in the government risk register were given a government risk ID and re-evaluated as a government risk. Moving forward, the contractor-identified risks fed into the Government risk management process as candidate risks to be fully re-evaluated, including risk title, statement, risk score, handling plan, etc.

This approach resulted in heavy government ownership in the risk management process as it relates to risks to all project requirements—regardless of the source of risk. This allowed the government to better understand and manage the project’s complete risk profile, including the relative ranking and handling of risks. Removing the contractor from the project risk management boards allowed for more candid discussion and decision.

The updated standard process associated with the final approach outlined was incorporated into center procedure.

 

Lesson(s) Learned

Approach 1 made it difficult to understand the complete risk profile of the project because risks were split between the contractor and the government risk registers. Subsequently, approach 2 provided greater ongoing awareness to the risks owned by the contractor; however, it did not result in significant improvement to the government mitigation of those risks. Disagreements on the assessment, handling, or closure of risks owned by the contractor were at times strained. Finally, approach 3 placed the responsibility on the government to identify, track, and assess handling plans for all risks to the project, utilizing the contractor process more as a source of information rather than the responsible authority for instrument development risk management.  This change resulted in an increase to Government tracked risks but allowed the government to better manage and understand the project risk posture.

Recommendation(s)

Projects should consider RBI's risk management approach 3 as a best practice for performing project risk management on a contracted instrument development project. It is also recommended to develop a standard risk management Data Requirement Description (DRD) that outlines specific elements of risk and risk management process or dedicated risk activities to which the contractor must adhere to ensure the process delivers the intended results: for instance, requiring a series of dedicated risk assessments associated with a list of topics such as suppliers, subcontractors, modules, assembly, integration, test, etc. The government should also consider performing formal assessments of a contractor’s risk management process after awarding the contract to ensure that the contractor risk management process is sufficient and well-understood, paying particular attention to any corporate constraints to the process. This could guide how the government utilizes the contractor risk management process and data and could be facilitated with a standard checklist or criteria.
