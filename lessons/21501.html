NASA Lesson #21501: Projects Benefit from Lessons Learned on Previous Spaceflight Projects

Driving Event / Description

The Ranger series of impact probes were designed by the JPL to fly to the moon and intentionally collide with it; the objective was to return images of the surface that would aid in selection of possible Apollo landing sites. Beginning in 1961, Rangers 1 through 6 all failed in this mission, and JPL came under withering criticism. However, lessons that were learned from the failures led to a successful Ranger 7, 8, and 9. Recently, reflight of JPL missions has become more common: e.g., the Mars Science Laboratory (MSL) rover design is being used for the Mars 2020 rover, Jason 1 has been followed by Jason 2 and Jason 3, OCO to OCO-2 and OCO-3, etc. Even for flight systems with low inheritance, a planetary mission may learn much from a previous mission that flew to the same planet.

The Juno spacecraft, which successfully entered the orbit of Jupiter on July 4, 2016, has a primary goal of revealing Jupiter's formation and evolution by observing its gravity and magnetic fields, atmospheric dynamics, and composition. The Juno project manager ­said (Reference (1)) that the Juno team benefited from lessons learned on previous JPL missions, perhaps most notably Galileo because it was the only previous mission to orbit Jupiter, and Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO) because it was one of the more recent JPL spacecraft built by the Juno system subcontractor. The Galileo mission generated much useful data on radiation and challenges posed by the Jupiter environment. Galileo survived over 20 anomalies over the course of the mission caused by Jupiter's extreme radiation environment, and Juno’s planned Jupiter orbit (Figure 1) would expose it over the course of the mission to a radiation dose of more than 20 million rads. The Juno flight system features avionics similar to MRO’s, and they benefited from lessons that were learned by the MRO project when it overcame a variety of in-flight avionics issues (Reference (2)).

The Juno project manager points out (Reference (1)) that the Juno team probably learned more lessons from prior successes than from spacecraft faults. “Most processes and design and test documentation are largely built on successes, but a lot of them have been fine-tuned in response to failures,” he said. “In general, lessons learned were built on successes and enhanced by failures.” For example, a “well-constructed design review package” from a project’s PDR, CDR, or SIR will have captured lessons learned that can be accessed to provide insight into how a project handled key challenges during design and development. Juno itself kept a running list of significant lessons that were learned from designing systems to endure “the most hazardous planet in the solar system-- an environment that’s unparalleled in terms of the intensity of radiation and magnetic fields.”

Lesson(s) Learned

In addition to the technical lessons learned captured in Reference (3), it became clear following the successful July Fourth Jupiter encounter that the Juno project team could identify (Reference (1)) some key project management lessons learned:

  • Benefits and risk of an extended definition and planning phase. A 30-month Phase B (Preliminary Design Phase) gave the team time to complete design trades and mature requirements, but it also induced a lack of urgency that contributed to some late deliveries at the subsystem level. An article in NASA’s ASK magazine discusses how the team took good advantage of the extended Phase B.

  • Early and constant focus on payload accommodation. With nine instruments on Juno, payload accommodation on the spacecraft was critical. The payload and spacecraft teams used “roadshows,” multiple models, technical interchange meetings, workshops, and telecons to define and mature interfaces and build relationships that were essential to resolving integration and test problems later in the project life cycle.

  • Creation of cross-project working groups. Formation of cross-project working groups brought unified focus to environment definition, design requirements, design implementation, and integration and test both horizontally with the science, spacecraft, and payload teams and vertically with the system, subsystem, box, and component teams in order to complete development within schedule.

  • Maximum use of contractors’ existing documentation and processes. To improve efficiency and teaming, the project made maximum use of contractor and instrument providers’ existing documentation and processes, which were reviewed against project requirements and implementation approaches to identify and resolve gaps.

  • Use of resident engineers at contractor facility. Juno located several key JPL personnel at the contractor facility to work closely with spacecraft development and integration/test teams. They maintained an effective partnership with the contractor team and provided the needed level of insight into critical activities.

Recommendation(s)

Throughout the project lifecycle, project staff should review lessons learned in the NASA Lesson Learned Information System (LLIS) and from other sources (Reference (4)) to identify past incidents and related recommendations that are relevant to the current phase of the project. The objective is to identify mission success factors and to channel the project into making new mistakes—instead of repeating the old mistakes that recur when lessons are not adequately learned.

Evidence / Additional Context

JPL has referenced this lesson learned as additional rationale and guidance supporting Paragraph 5.24 (“Management Practices: Lessons Learned”) in the Jet Propulsion Laboratory standard “Flight Project Practices, Rev. 11,” JPL DocID 58032, January 4, 2017.
