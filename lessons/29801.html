NASA Lesson #29801: Best Practices for the Elemental Profiling of High-Purity Hydrazine

Lesson ID: #29801 (Database ID: 29801)
Date: 2020-12-14
Organization: nesc
Program Phase: Not Applicable
Topic / Category: Ground Operations, Launch Vehicle, Parts, Materials, & Processes, Spacecraft, Propulsion, Quality
Source: View Official NASA LLIS Entry (live system)

Driving Event / Description

Hypergolic propellants (e.g., hydrazine (N2H4)) and monomethylhydrazine (MMH) are used to power monopropellant and bipropellant propulsion systems across a wide variety of commercial, DoD, and NASA programs.  Detailed understanding of the hypergolic fluid properties and characteristics is critical to their successful use in short and long duration missions.  Investigations to better understand HPH manufacturing processes and the associated introduction of contaminants have been a priority for the HPH user community after the chemical reaction scheme employed to produce a precursor to HPH, which is used as the feedstock by the Defense Logistics Agency’s sole source HPH provider, changed in mid-2018.

Particular concern arose regarding the possible introduction of organic species (e.g., carbonaceous compounds) and elemental content (e.g., cadmium (Cd)) to the final HPH product, as this carries an increased risk of performance degradation and/or flow-path blockage to thruster system valves, softgoods and catalyst beds.

Analysis was completed to identify extraneous unknown carbonaceous materials present in current HPH in addition to comprehensive elemental profiling by four laboratories, based on NESC-developed analytical methodology, to develop a full elemental profile of the commodity in relation to heritage HPH stocks.

The elemental laboratory data from each of the four laboratories unexpectedly found varying and/or high levels of multiple elements outside of nominal laboratory-to-laboratory variation, which was confirmed by the Kennedy Space Center-Component Refurbishment & Chemical Analysis Laboratory.  The NESC concluded that, while there was variation in the elemental content of the samples, there was also an apparent inconsistent handling of samples within each of the four laboratories that, in some cases, led to widely varying elemental assay results.

Lesson(s) Learned

There was an unexpectedly wide variation in elemental assay results from the analysis of single batch-sourced samples from four laboratories. Further analysis revealed that the provided samples themselves exhibited variation, possibly as a result of pre-laboratory handling. However, in some cases, large discrepancies were determined to have been caused by differences in analytical procedures and methods at the laboratories themselves. Refinement of analytical methodology for HPH, or other hydrazine derivative, sample handling and processing, as well as the instrumental analysis methodology for extended elemental content, are essential to gaining accurate and equivalent results from multiple laboratories performing this type of analysis.

Recommendation(s)

The recommendations to prevent this lesson from being relearned in the future take the form of best practices for conducting the elemental analysis process and are tabulated below:

  • A.    Glassware usage should be minimized in all steps of the analytical process to minimize sample contamination.  This includes the evaporation vessel used in the non-volatile residue (NVR) step (platinum recommended); storing of water, acids, standards, reagents, etc., used for the NVR step, acid digestion, and inductively coupled plasma analysis. Polypropylene and polymethyl pentene are regarded as good storage materials for this application but other plastics are also suitable.
  • B.    Blanks for water and acid stock solutions used in sample preparation should be prepared alongside, and analyzed with, each batch of samples analyzed to ensure any contamination, particularly silicon, is accounted for from the process.
  • C.    When using platinum evaporation dishes, adequate cleaning between samples should be ensured; two method options are suggested:
    • i.    Option 1: Gently scrub the pan with a synthetic fiber scouring pad in deionized water to remove any visibly discolored areas.  When no more deposits are noted, rinse with 25% by volume concentrated nitric acid in deionized water, allowing the acid to sit in the pans for several minutes and swirling to ensure it has contacted the entire interior.  Complete a final rinse with deionized water, then heat in a muffle furnace at 800°C for 1 hour minimum.
    • ii.    Option 2: Thoroughly rinse the pan with 5% nitric acid in deionized water and gently scrub to remove any visibly discolored areas.  When no more deposits are noted, rinse the pan with deionized water and dry with lint-free wipes.  Place the pans in a chemical fume hood and rinse with hydrofluoroether HFE-7100 precision cleaning solvent. (Note: other non-polar volatile organic solvents may be used for cleaning if directed by the chemist in charge).  Give each pan a final rinse with the last solvent.  Gently blow any remaining liquid off the dishes with filtered gaseous nitrogen, then place the dishes in a 110°C oven for 30 minutes.  Prior to use for analysis, place the pans on a hot plate, then fill with 5% nitric acid and bring to a boil and hold at a boil for 5 minutes.  Cool the pans, and rinse with deionized water.
  • D.    Method detection limits and reporting limits should be established for all elements in analysis for proper reporting of trace elemental levels.
  • E.    For ICP-OES (Inductively Coupled Plasma - Optical Emission Spectrometry) or ICP-MS (Inductively Coupled Plasma - Mass Spectrometry) analysis, it is important to survey the elements that are to be analyzed and determine what possible interferences may exist for those elements, which should be addressed prior to analysis.
    • -    When using ICP-OES, inter-element correction factors should be established and routinely checked for accuracy with spiked standards for accurate quantification of all elements in the analysis.  An internal standard should always be used to ensure accurate quantification from any variation in instrument performance during operation. Yttrium is one such suitable internal standard option.
    • -    For ICP-MS, a suggested set up includes the ability to use helium mode, when needed, to remove polyatomic interferences.  If sample load is very low, calibration achieved by bracketing the sample(s) between check standards in lieu of using an internal standard is acceptable.  If sample load is high and regular, robust calibration and internal standard use is typical.
  • F.    Samples should be analyzed in duplicate or triplicate, when possible.  Preferable replicate relative percent difference levels are less than or equal to 20%.
