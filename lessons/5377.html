NASA Lesson #5377: Human Factors and the Space Flight Awareness (SFA) Employee Motivation and Recognition Program

Lesson ID: #5377 (Database ID: 5377)
Date: 2011-02-08
Organization: Kennedy Space Center
Program Phase: Not Applicable
Mission Directorate: Human Exploration and Operations
Source: View Official NASA LLIS Entry (live system)

Driving Event / Description

There was not a dedicated human factors section in the Apollo Program reference materials, and no formal human factors lessons learned. However, there were several methods used to analyze human factors and provide proper training for the human interaction with the hardware that was developed. The concepts were well defined, especially for the displays and controls requirements for the spacecraft. There was a significant emphasis on 0-G human factor design and testing of these interfaces that dealt with the flight crew, and this led to a well-designed interface for the crew. Ground Support Equipment (GSE) safety and other areas dealing with the 1-G operations were important as well, but were not maximized through formal human factors principles. To mitigate risk during ground processing, the SFA Program originated during the Mercury Program. Since then, all U.S. human space projects to date have utilized the concept of the SFA Program as a way to mitigate risk.

Lesson(s) Learned

SFA uses a variety of motivational awards and incentives along with active education to remind the Government and industry team of their role in achieving flight safety and mission success. Training and motivation are important for the human element to perform at their best with any system. However, we must first start with the optimal system, then enhance the system with the appropriate training. Ongoing human factors analysis contributions during design of the spacecraft and associated ground systems are a key component to how flight safety and mission success can be achieved. With the new technologies and untried methods, and the importance that spacecraft processing operations play in the success of each mission, more emphasis in human factors will be required. The 0-G human factors practices for the flight crew are well in place and will be easily accepted in future programs, but 1-G human factors for the ground personnel will need special attention because there has not been an emphasis on this in previous spacecraft development.

Recommendation(s)

  1. In the use and development of space systems, launch vehicles, crewed vehicles, and the ground systems to process and launch these vehicles increases, ensure that the new designs are assessed by a human factors engineer.
  2. Establish and provide proper training for new technology and systems to reduce confusion and human error.
  3. Continue to use the SFA program as a training tool.
  4. Ensure coordination between the flight and ground crew, which is essential to mission success.
  5. Ensure that the SFA program addresses the usefulness of human factors.
  6. Incorporate Human Systems Integration (HIS) with the flight and ground human factors engineering to leverage the methods and experience gained by the flight human factors expert into the ground human factors analysis methods.
