Driving Event / Description

The initial design of the HyperSpectral Instrument for Climate Science (HySICS) Pointing System (HPS) within the Climate Absolute Radiance and Refractivity Observatory (CLARREO) Pathfinder payload utilized dynamically flexed, multi-layer flex harnesses in an “Omega” configuration for transmission of electrical signals and power between elements of the payload throughout the pointing system range of motion.  The HPS provides rotation of the HySICS instrument boresight about an azimuthal axis and elevation axis; thus two separate dynamically flexed harnesses – one azimuth and one elevation - both with “Omega” configurations, were incorporated into the pointing system design.  Figure 1 shows a representation of the multi-layer flex harness in its intended operational configuration.  Figure 2 shows a representation of one ribbon within the flex harness laid flat; the semi-circular region forms the “Omega” portion of the ribbon.  Figure 3 shows the installed flex harness in the azimuth gimbal.  The semi-circular portion experiences dynamic flexing via “rolling” as the harness rotates about the center of the semi-circle.  Figure 4 shows the installation orientation of the elevation flex harness.  The azimuth flex harness contained six individual multilayer copper/Kapton tapes; the elevation flex harness contained four individual multi-layer copper/Kapton tapes.  Individual tapes within each harness were not physically bonded together.  Small mechanical retaining clamps were installed near the ends of the “omega” flexing region to clamp the harness, through slight mechanical pressure, to the fixed and rotating elements of the harness structural channel.  Rotation of the rotating element of the structural channel (e.g., Figure 3, semi-transparent top surface) would thus result in a “rolling” flexural motion of the “omega” portion of the harness within the channel.

 

 

  Within the flexing/rolling region, the individual flex tapes were layered but not bonded, which allowed for some small amount of sliding of the tapes relative to each other as the flexing region “rolled” throughout the rotational range of motion.  Because the individual tapes were not bonded together, each individual tape retained its own neutral bending axis.  Each tape was comprised of outer Kapton layers, a copper trace layer, a middle Kapton layer, and a copper crosshatch-pattern shield layer.  Figure 5 shows the single tape layer stackup for the original design. Note the copper layers are not at the neutral bending axis (middle) of the tape, which causes increased strain and fatigue on the copper during bending. The same basic flex harness design had previously been successfully life tested to approximately 2000 flex cycles for the Total and Solar Irradiance Sensor-1 (TSIS-1) project and flown in the TSIS-1 payload.  On orbit, the TSIS-1 flex harnesses experienced dynamic flexing only once during the mechanism deployment phase during payload commissioning.

The initial CLARREO Pathfinder HPS flex harness design was derived from the TSIS-1 flex harness design in consideration of the prior life test for TSIS-1.  The minimum flexural life needed for the CLARREO Pathfinder harnesses was 15,000 cycles, equivalent to 1x mission life.  A life test to 2x mission life (30,000 cycles) was planned in the project schedule but was delayed in execution due to multiple factors, including necessary maturation of the flex harness design, limited number of vendors with the capability to fabricate the harnesses, lengthy production schedules, and COVID-19.  A life test of the HPS azimuth flex harness life test unit was initiated in summer 2020 after the project’s Critical Design Review and an extended facility shutdown due to COVID-19.  Figure 6 shows a photograph of the life test fixture with test harness installed.  Copper traces within the life test harness began exhibiting inconsistent and high resistance values starting at approximately 2000 cycles, indicating signs of early failure.  Under continued flexing additional copper traces exhibited high resistance/open circuit.  The life test was deemed a test failure and stopped at 20,000 cycles, at which time nearly all coppr traces exhibited signs of failure.

​The CLARREO Pathfinder project initiated an investigation into the HPS flex harness life test failure mechanism(s) and harness design.  Technical assistance was sought from the NASA Engineering and Safety Center (NESC) who contributed personnel with expertise that greatly assisted in the investigation.  The investigation lasted approximately five months concluding in January 2021.  Multiple non-destructive evaluation (NDE), destructive physical analysis (DPA), and analytical techniques were used to identify and characterize mechanisms contributing to the failure of copper traces within the flex harness.  The root cause of the copper trace failures was fatigue cracking of the copper signal traces predominantly in locations that were overlapped by the crosshatch copper traces within the shield layer.  A factor contributing to the copper trace failures was that each flex tape within the harness was a “double-sided” layup configuration where the copper signal trace layer was located away from the neutral bending axis of the tape, causing increased mechanical strain and fatigue on the traces during flexing.

 

The investigation produced multiple recommendations for a redesigned flex harness and life testing methodology.  Key recommendations for redesign were:

  1. Use single-sided copper ribbons with the copper as close as possible to the neutral plane
  2. Use wider copper traces than the original design. Consider 30 mils. Use modeling to validate if ½ oz copper is the best option for meeting the life test requirement for the flex ribbon harness.
  3. Add IPC 2223 dynamic flex requirements to design requirements (minimum flex cycles per IPC test method
  4. Add a second vendor source
  5. Assess CLARREO on-orbit operational requirements to determine the life test parameters
  6. Include temperature extremes in the life test and if possible vacuum conditions.

Many of the NESC’s recommendations were adopted and incorporated into the redesigned HPS azimuth and elevation flex harnesses and life testing plan.  A redesigned azimuth flex harness successfully passed a 4X life test (60,000 cycles) in December 2021.  Test cycles were completed at ambient, hot operational, and cold operational temperatures at atmospheric pressure (not vacuum).  Key design aspects for the redesigned flex tapes comprising the harnesses were:

  • Single-sided flex tape layup construction with copper trace layer located in the center of the tape, at the neutral bending axis, as shown in Figure 7.
  • Elimination of crosshatch copper shield layer within the tapes.  A separate tape consisting of only the copper crosshatch pattern was added to the harness for shielding purposes.
  • Single-sided copper clad polyimide substrate.  Consists of 2-mil Kapton coverlay and 0.7 mil (e.g. ½ oz.) copper layer
  • ½-oz. Rolled Highly Annealed Copper, minimum 30 mil trace width
  • Kapton® coverlay 1 mil thick, 1 mil acrylic coverlay adhesive
  • No part identification markings (i.e., silk-screened lettering) in the flexing portion of the tape (no such markings existed in the flexing portion of the tape in the original design, but restated here due to importance)

Lesson(s) Learned

Dynamically flexed flex tapes and/or harnesses should be considered an engineering development activity in project formulation, particularly for tapes/harnesses that will experience high cycle fatigue with cycle counts ≥ 500.  Therefore, projects should plan to develop test coupons and prototypes and conduct testing at representative strain levels and life cycle counts early in the project, ideally Phase B.  These early tests can help to evaluate the design principles, the selected material system, and vendor fabrication capabilities.  Identifying and using more than one vendor provides additional options.  

Recommendation(s)

Some specific flex-tape recommendations for dynamically flexed tapes include:

  • Keep the copper traces as close to the neutral bending axis as possible.
    • Whenever possible, use single-sided flex layup construction (i.e., Kapton – Copper – Kapton) with the copper trace layer located at the neutral bending axis of the tape.  
    • Ensure there is no part identification markings (e.g., silk-screened lettering) in the flexing region of the tape/harness.  The presence of silk-screened lettering can locally skew the location of the neutral bending axis, and the edges of the silk-screen lettering can act as stress concentrators that could lead to localized copper trace failures.  
    • If multi-layer “double-sided” flex tape layups must be used, avoid having copper traces overlap each other.  This results in an “I-beam” effect that increases mechanical strain on the traces which may cause copper trace fracture (see Figure 7-11 in Fjelstad, J., Flexible Circuit Technology, 4th Edition).  
  • High-purity, rolled, annealed copper is generally preferred for high-cycle flexural applications.  If rolled annealed copper is selected, be aware that rolled annealed copper typically exhibits anisotropy in the copper grain structure due to the rolling process. This anisotropy is often identified using the convention of “Machine” vs. “Cross-Machine” directions, referring to the direction in which the copper was rolled in the rolling machine.  For high cycle dynamic flexing applications rolled annealed copper should be oriented such that the “Machine” direction runs parallel to the copper traces. 
  • Flex tapes/harness designs configured in a “clock spring” configuration may be preferable to tapes/harnesses in an “Omega” configuration as the clock spring design may offer lower mechanical strain on the copper traces.  
  • When life testing, ensure the test is representative of the in-service conditions expected including range and rate of motion as well as environmental conditions.  NASA-STD-5001B specifies a service life factor of 4.0 be applied for fatigue life assessments.  For very-high-cycle applications that would make life testing prohibitively long in duration, fatigue cycle tests of coupons in a flexural testing machine (see IPC-TM-650 Section 2.4.3.1) can be used to construct a Manson-Coffin plot to evaluate predicted fatigue life.
  • When a copper trace failure occurs as indicated by an increase in resistance or open circuit, thermographic imaging of the traces with current flowing through them has been demonstrated to be an effective method for quickly diagnosing the approximate location of the break.  Additional diagnostic methods that have been used successfully are XRay/Computed Tomography (CT) imaging and Time Domain Reflectometry.
