NASA Lesson #30101: Cable Harness Wiring and Connector Anomalies Caused by Induced Damage in Human Spaceflight Vehicles

Driving Event / Description

NASA has found that the commercial spacecraft developers and operators have a reduced incidence of wiring anomalies compared to the Space Shuttle and has identified causes for this reduced incidence.  This reduction can potentially be attributed to:

  1. Limited re-use compared to the SSP
  2. Reduced exposed wiring in modern spacecraft routing designs
  3. New vehicles being integrated in a single orientation, allowing better access control, and enable ground crew to avoid sensitive areas and causing collateral damage
  4. The reduced size of ground crew and scope of processing
  5. Less wire, smaller length of wire 
  6. New insulations that replaced Kapton
  7. Use of Scoop Proof MIL-DTL-38999 connectors to prevent damaging pins during connector mating
  8. System Separation
  9. System Architecture (avionics box consolidation and greater incorporation of printed circuit boards (PCB) to eliminate wiring
  10. Lower Level of Inspections and Reporting/Inspection Criteria
  11. Reduced testing scope for the detection of post-installation failures

Additionally, as NASA moves away from legacy quality standards to industry consensus standards and commercial providers transition to a re-use production environment with larger systems and ground crews, there is a greater risk of increase incidence of wire and harness anomalies pre-launch. A consequence of the move to industry standards is the loss of user post-installation testing with reliance on manufacturing testing alone. NASA can offer relevant wire anomaly experience to help manage the incidence of wiring anomalies.

Lesson(s) Learned

As a result of many years of expensive lessons learned, NASA imposed electrical tests after installation in locations where it was determined that the majority of cable / wiring harness damage occurs during installation and maintenance actions. The probability of incurring immediate or latent damage depends on the robustness of the cable / harness assembly and the installation itself. Installation of a cable / harness assembly into an avionics box would not be expected to carry as much risk as installation / routing of the cable / harness assembly through the bulkheads and wiring trays of a large vehicle.  NASA has discovered wiring damage issues in every crewed vehicle system (Apollo, Shuttle, ISS, and now Orion) as well as some issues in commercial spacecraft developers and operators’ vehicles.

 

While it is agreed that post-installation electrical testing takes time (and by extension

increased cost), it is often less difficult (and less costly) to find and repair damage BEFORE vehicle integration proceeds to the point that access is no longer possible without disassembly of the vehicle.  Functional testing may not identify a degraded signal path or latent defect condition.   Applying a high voltage does have a high probability of locating a degraded dielectric path or latent defect.   Of the three (3) tests listed in NASA-STD-8739.4, Rev A, Workmanship Standard for Crimping, Interconnecting Cables, Harnesses, and Wiring, [18.2.8], the two (2) most commonly used are (in sequence):

(1) Continuity and Shorts, and

(2) Insulation Resistance (IR).

These electrical tests will not identify a potential short circuit that may occur during the dynamic loading phase of a flight if there is no detectable amount of current leakage during test – or if the test wasn’t required because the wiring had been previously tested. The most notorious example is the loss of two main engine control units during launch of STS-93 due to a wire in the payload bay rubbing against the sharp edge of a screw head and shorting as it moved under launch load. This was a close call.

Specific lessons learned on post installation testing are:

  1. All boxes, mounted or not, should be isolated from the potential harmful effects of the test voltages.  Ensure avionics boxes will not be affected by high voltage testing by not connecting harnesses to mounted boxes.
  2. Verify the system complies with NASA Standard 8739.4 for assembly and integration: NASA-STD-8739.4, unless tailored, requires the integrators to perform dielectric withstanding voltage (DWV) tests on all harnesses after installation. This requirement is imposed to identify potential damage caused during the installation of the harnesses and provides guidance on applying IR and DWV to wire harnesses.
  3. Application of post installation testing Is especially important in difficult-to-access areas where physical access to perform visual inspection is hampered, high traffic areas, and where harnesses are manipulated to install them.
  4. Access to the harness connectors and to the harnesses themselves drives the integration approach to provide field joints to simplify testing, eliminate very long test harnesses, and excessive manipulation of the harnesses to access them safely. If excessive manipulations of the harnesses are required, then the benefit of the post installation testing is negated due to the additional handling and potential to damage the harnesses during testing.

NASA’s has not yet seen the same incidence of discrepancies in the new generation of vehicles as was seen in the historical data of discrepancies experience on Apollo and Space Shuttle.  The Space Shuttle has a complex wiring system with approximately 600 harnesses, 7,000 connectors, 120,000 wire segments and 228 miles of wire.  NASA’s experience shows handling-induced damage or damage resulting from work in close proximity is a primary source of wiring anomalies.  Figure 1 shows a breakdown of the types of wire related problems encountered in the Orbiter fleet.  The types of wire related problems shown include chafe protection, Kapton damage, jacket damage, shield damage, exposed conductor, and conductor damage.

NASA did conduct a lessons-learned review after the Space Shuttle was retired and the following observation were made with respect to the electrical wiring system:

  • It is important to perform detailed closeout inspection after work on cables or connectors.
  • Wiring is easily damaged by nearby work activity.
  • Training of personnel is critical both to prevent damage and to detect damage.
  • Wire chafe protection should be used to protect critical* wiring.
  • Separate critical* redundant wire runs and/or install barrier materials to protect against the effects of arc tracking.
  • The effects of aging, operation, environmental exposure, re-use should be factored into the expected operational life of the wiring system

* Critical wires are wires that, should they fail, pose a catastrophic hazard (loss of crew risk or loss of mission) risk

 

Finally, it should be noted that several high-profile failures in NASA history have included wiring faults and handling-induced damage as related causes.  Included in these are the Apollo 1 and Apollo 13 incidents.

The Findings for the Apollo 1 fire, which resulted in the death of the crew, was that it is most probable that the fire was initiated by an electric arc and was most probably brought about by some minor malfunction or failure of equipment or wire insulation. The components involved had a history of many removals and of technical difficulties with design and installation features making removal or repair difficult. In addition, deficiencies in design, manufacture, installation, rework and quality control existed in the Teflon electrical wiring.  The report conclusion included a recommendation that rigorous inspection at all stages of wiring design, manufacture, and installation be enforced.

Similarly, on Apollo 13, the No. 2 oxygen tank, had been previously installed in the service module of Apollo 10, but was removed for modification (and was damaged in the process of removal). At 55 hours 55 minutes, oxygen tank No. 2 catastrophically failed, causing No. 1 tank also to fail. The Apollo 13 command module’s normal supply of electricity, light, and water was lost when they were about 200,000 miles from Earth.  Subsequent investigation of the accident determined the cause of the combustion was most probably the ignition of Teflon wire insulation on the fan motors wires, caused by electric arcs in this wiring.  In both cases, handling-induced damage as well as wire insulation failure were contributors.

The current commercial spacecraft developers and operators’ space vehicles appear to benefit from more simplified designs and lower technician traffic during integration.  In the case of the larger NASA vehicles, ground processing necessarily involved non-wire-oriented staff whose integration work did not appreciate the susceptibilities for wire damage.

 

Recommendation(s)

As commercial vehicles and other NASA vehicles (e.g. Orion SLS) that return to earth and undergo maintenance proceed into the production and re-use of human-rated vehicles, and as the wiring system grows in these designs, shared lessons learned from NASA regarding wire anomalies can aid in the prevention of failures.  This includes awareness of human factors such as ease of access, harness routing for better installation/maintenance access, and proximity to obstructions. Additionally, employ harness design practices that minimize single point failures and conduct IR and DWV tests on installed harnesses.
