Driving Event / Description

James K. Erickson of the NASA/Caltech Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) is the project manager post-launch for the Mars Science Laboratory (MSL) project. During Phase E, he also served as project manager for the Galileo mission to Jupiter, project manager for the Mars Exploration Rovers (MER), and project manager for the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO) project, as well as worked on a number of other JPL spaceflight projects. On July 19, 2016, he gave a Pause & Learn presentation (Reference (1)) to a meeting of JPL project managers on “The Value of Doing Nothing.”

During his service as the project manager on the four successful NASA “flagship” missions, Jim recalled experiencing 50 flight system entries into safe mode on 5 spacecraft/probes—Mars Science Laboratory (MSL), Spirit, Opportunity, MRO, and Galileo. Of these 50 anomalies, only the MSL Sol 200 Anomaly (Reference (2)) and perhaps a couple others were truly emergencies that had to be dealt with promptly. For example, doing nothing was not an option for the Sol 200 Anomaly; but when the Opportunity rover became stuck in the Martian sand in May 2005 (Reference (3)), JPL had months to resolve the issue.

Some vehicles experienced recurrent anomalies including some that, in retrospect, could have been predicted:

  • Galileo experienced 29 safe mode entries, to the point where the project even developed a software patch that “ignored” one particular type of reset. It was related to the radiation environment and occurred during near approaches to Jupiter.

  • MRO experienced several categories of resets that would occur periodically.

Jim explained that for each of the safe mode anomalies, “at least initially I had the sick feeling in the pit of my stomach that this might be ‘the one’.”

Jim suggests that the foremost job of a project manager during a putative operational emergency is to inform and manage up the chain of authority-- both at JPL and NASA HQ. “Managing” in this context refers to insulating the project team that is working the technical problem from the distractions caused by outside parties’ interest in the anomaly.  All interactions with JPL management, NASA, and the media should pass through the project manager.

Lesson(s) Learned

Much value can be derived from a planned and consistent response to a putative in-flight emergency. A project manager should exhibit a steady, calm demeanor in all interactions with upper management, and most importantly, with the project team. This is because the project team members take their cues from the project leadership.  If the project manager exhibits confidence in the team, both the team members and JPL upper management feel confident. 

In contrast, allowing panic to spread within the project and to upper management is unlikely to elicit the optimal response, and may be counter-productive. 

Recommendation(s)

  1. What to do first:
    • Make sure your team is properly notified that there is an issue!

    • Make sure your pre-identified anomaly contacts are beginning to work the problem:
      • If not, get them engaged.
    • Review (at least mentally) your anomaly response procedure(s).

    • Make sure your response team is organized, with an anomaly lead to serve as the PM’s point of contact.

    • Take your first pulse on the anomaly:
      • Is this a limited impact, slowly unfolding, issue?
      • Is the vehicle taking care of itself?  Did the flight system’s autonomous fault protection respond to the anomaly properly?
      • Does downlinked health data indicate that the vehicle status is degrading? Degrading rapidly?
  2. What to do next:
    • Identify the basics about your vehicle state, even if it is tentative, or you have limited knowledge.
      • Be aware that you must cope with limited information flow from the spacecraft. For example, at the 10 bps emergency mode, Galileo telemetry took a long time to generate all the information necessary for complete state knowledge. Mars surface assets may have only orbital relay capability, or extremely limited direct-to-Earth downlink.
    • Communicate what you know upwards, even if all you can do is phone your JPL boss and the NASA HQ contact and give them a 2-minute rundown.
      • Keeping communications flowing to your superiors is vital, particularly in the rapid-pace social media climate.
      • You don’t want your boss to be blindsided by media that have been tipped to an issue by bloggers.
      • An e-mail to the JPL Director is not sufficient, as he may be asleep or in a press conference where he is about to be blindsided by a blogger. Also, let the Director know specifically when you plan to provide more information.
      • Write a crisp concise summary to follow a phone chat. Figure 1 (Reference (4)) is a sample that was sent to the Mars Program Manager, the NASA Principal Investigator, and senior MSL project staff.
      • Make sure your team (including all science members) knows what is going on. On one flagship mission, the mission scientists weren't aware the spacecraft was in safe mode until they realized they hadn't been receiving their science return.
      • When distributing anomaly information, be sensitive to ITAR/EAR restrictions.

  • Curiosity is healthy, stable, and currently operating nominally in safe mode.

  • On July 2 [2016] at about 3:00 pm PDT, Curiosity did not communicate with MRO as expected.  Afterwards, an Odyssey pass did not occur, and the team began listening with the DSN [NASA Deep Space Network] for safe mode carrier, and detected it.

  • Subsequently, at about 2:00 am July 3, the MSL team received telemetry via MRO that indicated we are in surface standby, with nothing marked unhealthy by Curiosity’s prime computer, the Rover Compute Element B (RCE-B), in safe mode. Flight software seems to indicate it asserted a Fatal interrupt, but subsequent expected re–boots occurred normally.  Fault protect seems to indicate at least one reset, and possibly more.  On the first look with limited data, Power looks nominal, bus balance indicates the RTG [Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator] short is still in place as expected.

  • With limited data, it appears that flight software detected a error in a transaction between the Malin Science Camera (MastCAM - MCAM) [Mast Camera] and the RCE.  The indication was that the data write from the MCAM was attempted in a different location than the RCE gave permission to MCAM to write in.  At this time, there is no indication that any hardware was involved in the error, but more analysis is required. Commanding to retrieve more data from the vehicle related to the area of interest is under consideration.

  1. Continuing actions:
    • Keep an eye on the pace of the anomaly. Monitor the incident continuously so that you understand the time remaining to analyze the situation and to make a decision on a course of action.

    • There is much value in “doing nothing” when an improper action may be irreversible.
      • Trust (but verify) your fault protection.  Remember that you tested it extensively prior to launch-- in an environment that wasn’t adrenalin charged.
      • Avoid “go fever”!  No decision may be superior to a decision that was timely but bad.
      • If you have a stable vehicle, you can afford to wait and ensure that all of your telemetry supports the conclusions you have made about the anomaly.
    • Continue reporting status to upper management.
      • The frequency of communication depends on the pace of the anomaly.
      • In the MSL Sol 200 anomaly, the pace was extremely rapid as the situation unfolded.
