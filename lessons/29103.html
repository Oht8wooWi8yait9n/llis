Driving Event / Description

A copper tube was pinched as a test for the Mass Spectrometer observing lunar operations (MSolo) Calibration Gas System (CGS). A CT scan revealed that a pinch that was seemingly acceptable later separated to form a leak path.

Lesson(s) Learned

The pinch was initially visually inspected and helium leak checked and no visual anomalies or leaks were detected. After a CT scan revealed that the seemingly acceptable pinch  separated after the helium leak check to form a leak path, an investigation was started to try to form an empirical method for verifying that a pinch has cold welded correctly. The team first turned to industry experts on copper tube pinching. While they offered a plethora of tips and tricks to try to get the best pinch possible, they were not able to provide an empirical method to ensure that a flight pinch is sealed correctly. The MSolo team then performed CT scans and inverted metallography on several copper tube pinches in an attempt to find measurements on the pinch cross-section that could be taken to distinguish a “good” pinch from a “bad” one. The results were documented, but they varied and were not useful for definitively deeming a pinch to be acceptable or not. The CT scans and inverted metallography did, however, provide insight into the shape of the pinch. A pinch with a nominal shape is more likely to have cold welded correctly. This was useful in conjunction with how the operator “felt” the pinch went. The pinch tool operator pinched several dozen practice samples and could tell from experience when a pinch didn't feel right. When some of those samples were CT scanned, the scan showed an off-nominal shape. An off-nominal shape does not guarantee that the pinch did not cold weld but when conducting our final flight pinch, we re-pinched to increase the chances of flying a successful pinch with a nominal shape.

The team also took into account some lessons learned from copper tube pinch failures for spaceflight applications in the past. The pinch point is delicate and can potentially leak if impacted. These lessons learned urged those using pinched copper tubes in spaceflight applications to protect the pinch point of the copper tube during transit and in-flight, if possible.

Recommendation(s)

  • Have pinch tool operator perform several practice pinches until he/she has an intuitive feel for the pinch tool kickback that occurs for a quotgoodquot pinch versus a quotbadquot pinch.
  • Perform CT scans and/or inverted metallography to observe the shape of the pinch.
  • Use some means of protection for the pinch point during transit and in-flight, if possible.
